Most robots have no personality, so I'm building one that does. The platform is a ballbot: a self-balancing robot on an inverted pendulum architecture, the kind of thing that has to actively fight gravity every millisecond just to stand still. Keeping it upright is a controls problem. Making it feel alive is a different problem layered on top, and the second one is the whole reason I'm doing this.

## Staying upright is the easy part

I say "easy" with a straight face only because it's the well-understood part. An inverted pendulum is unstable by definition, so balance runs as a cascaded control loop on an NVIDIA Jetson Orin: an outer loop that watches the body's pitch and decides how the wheels should move, and an inner loop that actually tracks that wheel velocity.

```python
# cascaded loop: outer balance drives the inner velocity target
angle_pid = PID(kp=..., ki=..., kd=...)   # hold body pitch near upright
wheel_pid = PID(kp=..., ki=..., kd=...)   # track the velocity balance asks for

def control_step(state, dt):
    # outer: how fast should the wheels go to stay balanced?
    target_velocity = angle_pid.update(error=state.pitch, dt=dt)
    # inner: drive the motors to hit that velocity
    torque = wheel_pid.update(error=target_velocity - state.wheel_velocity, dt=dt)
    return clamp(torque, -TORQUE_LIMIT, TORQUE_LIMIT)
```
Tuned well, this keeps the body upright and lets the robot drive around. On its own, though, it's just a very busy way to stand still.

## Personality is a layer, not a hack

The character lives above the controller. Instead of bolting expressions onto a balance loop, the behavior layer treats the whole body as something to perform with: it leans toward a person it's tracking, settles when things are calm, adds a little wobble when something surprises it, and points its "gaze" where its attention is. None of that overrides balance. It feeds the controller new targets and lets the balance loop figure out how to honor them without falling over.

That separation is the trick. The character decides what it wants to express; the controller owns physics.
## Keeping the two from fighting

The failure mode is obvious once you've seen it: the personality layer asks for a motion the balance loop can't safely produce, and the robot either ignores it (looks dead) or tips (looks broken). So the character layer issues setpoints inside a safety envelope, and the controller always has the last word. An expressive lean is a request, not a command. Believability that respects the physics is the only believability that survives contact with the real world.

## What's next

Right now the focus is the control stack and the first character behaviors: gaze, lean, and reactions that read as intentional rather than twitchy. After that, richer social behaviors, so the robot doesn't just stay upright, it reacts to the people around it. I want a robot that feels alive, not one that merely refuses to fall down.
